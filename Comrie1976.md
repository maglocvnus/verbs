## Chapter 4. *Aspect and tense*

### 4.4. *Combined tense/aspect oppositions*

\[pp. 78–82\]

In written <mark>Arabic</mark>, every finite verb form is inflectionally either *Perfect* or *Imperfect* (and neither of these forms can be said to derive from the other).
Importantly, the meaning distinction between these two forms cannot be defined solely in terms of either aspect or tense, but requires reference to both:
- Perfect = perfective aspect AND past tense (relative to the temporal focus)
- Imperfect = imperfective aspect OR non-past tense (relative to the temporal focus).

Some further details:
1. In temporal isolation (ie. the finite verb is neither modified by a temporal adverbial nor subordinate to another finite verb):
  - the temporal focus is the *moment of utterance*
  - non-past tense is *present tense*.
2. When modified by a temporal adverbial which has past tense relative to the moment of utterance (eg. ‘yesterday’, ‘last Friday’): ie. past temporal focus?
  - a non-subordinate Imperfect verb is coerced into having (absolute) *past* imperfective meaning
  - \[<mark>MM</mark>: Is this not just *relative present*, relative to the absolute past time of the adverbial?\]

However, when modified by an (absolute) future temporal adverbial:
- a non-subordinate Imperfect verb form may be coerced into having (absolute) *future perfective* meaning.

In other words, an Arabic sentence with a surface meaning like ‘God be judging them tomorrow’ can be interpreted as ‘God will judge them tomorrow’.

This means that the difference between the Arabic Perfect and Imperfect is *not* simply a matter of perfective versus imperfective aspect.



Perfect: (absolute) past perfective
Imperfect: everything else – imperfective meaning and/or (absolute) non-past meaning



Moreover, when a non-subordinate Imperfect verb *X* is temporally modified by a *when*-clause headed by a subordinate Perfect verb *Y*:
- *Y* can have *absolute future* meaning.

In other words, an Arabic sentence with a surface meaning like ‘I be coming when the date ripened’ can be interpreted as ‘I will come when the date has ripened.’ 

This means that the difference between the Arabic Perfect and Imperfect is not purely a matter of absolute past versus absolute non-past tense – a Perfect verb can have both absolute past and absolute non-past meaning.



Can the difference between the Arabic Perfect and Imperfect be a matter of past versus non-past *relative* tense?

Perfect – past perfective meaning (incl. relative to a future event)
Imperfect – everything else, ie. past and non-past imperfective, future perfective.

### 4.5. *Aspect and time reference in tenseless languages*

\[pp. 82–4]]

<mark>Yoruba</mark>, <mark>Igbo</mark> (West African):
- Some verbs are lexically imperfective (ie. ‘stative’ verbs).
- Other verbs are lexically perfective (ie. ‘non-stative’ verbs).
- There is an imperfective marker (Yoruba *ń*, Igbo *ná*) which can precede a lexically perfective verb, yielding a progressive/habitual construction 
- There are no explicit tense markers, but by default (eg. in the absence of a temporal adverbial):
  - Imperfective forms have **present** time reference.
  - Perfective forms have **past** time reference.

<mark>Proto-Indo-European</mark> may have worked in a similar way:
- There were two basic verbal categories – imperfective and perfective (traditionally known as ‘present’ and ‘aorist’ respectively).
- Imperfective verbs had default present time reference; perfective verbs had default past time reference.
- The so-called ‘imperfect’ verb forms were a later development, usually involving adding some kind of explicit past tense marker to an imperfective verb, hence the characterisation of the imperfect as ‘present in the past’.
